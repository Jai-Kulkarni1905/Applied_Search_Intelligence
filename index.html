Which pages should an SEO team review first?

Testing whether a Random Forest can improve a simple rule for deciding which pages deserve attention first.

Author Jai KulkarniLane Refresh Opportunity ScoringDate 29 August 2026Unit page within client
Main finding

The simpler baseline gave the better review order.

The Random Forest found useful signal about later movement, but the baseline had higher Precision@K on every checkpoint shown here. The model was not strong enough to justify replacing the simpler rule.

Abstract

A content team may have thousands of pages in its inventory, but only enough time to review a small number of them. I started with that practical problem: if only a limited number of pages can be checked, which ones should be looked at first? Using pseudonymized FlyRank data, I built a transparent score from search performance during 1 to 15 March 2026 and used the following 16 days to observe whether impressions actually fell. I then trained a Random Forest on the same information and asked it to produce its own ranking. The model did find useful signal and performed better than the 0.49 held out base rate, but the simple baseline produced the stronger ranking on the main test set, so the final recommendation is to keep the baseline as the review queue and use the model as supporting evidence rather than adding complexity without a measurable gain.

1. The problem

Which pages should be reviewed first?

This is a small question, but it matters when a site has more pages than a content team can inspect carefully. The goal is not to decide automatically that a page needs a rewrite. The goal is to put the pages most worth investigating near the top of a queue.

That queue can save review time. A practitioner can start with the first page, look at the signals that caused it to rank highly, check the surrounding search context and then decide whether the right action is a refresh, another form of investigation or no action at all.

Research questionCan a learned model produce a better order for this review queue than a transparent rule based score?

What was left out

  • Future outcome fields were used only after ranking to construct and evaluate the outcome.
  • Trend fields such as trend_direction and trend_pct were not model inputs because they describe the movement being measured.
  • Pseudonymous IDs were used for joins, grouping and validation, not as predictive features.
  • Existing product decisions were excluded so the model could not simply learn an existing FlyRank decision.

3. Methodology

The baseline

The baseline is intentionally easy to inspect. It estimates expected CTR within average position buckets and combines four observable components: CTR need, visibility strength, position need and zero visibility need. The resulting score gives each page a place in the review queue and the reason code tells the reviewer what signal caused the page to be surfaced.

The Random Forest

The Random Forest uses nine inputs from 1 to 15 March: impressions, clicks, average position, organic sessions, AI sessions, CTR, encoded position bucket, expected CTR for the position bucket and average daily impressions. Client and content identifiers remain available for context and grouping, but they are not model features.

The outcome

A page is labelled is_declining = 1 when its average daily impressions from 16 to 31 March are lower than its average daily impressions from 1 to 15 March. This lets the model learn from something that happened after the ranking was made.

Validation

The primary comparison uses GroupShuffleSplit with the client as the group. Thirty clients are used for training and thirteen unseen clients are held out for testing. Both rankings are evaluated on the same test pages. Precision@K is used because the real output is a queue and a reviewer normally works from the top down.

Leakage check

The leakage audit was completed before the model results were interpreted. It checked the feature and outcome windows, future fields, derived target information and product decision fields. The modeling workflow also checked that the future period was not present in the feature dataframe.

The held out base rate was 0.49. The Random Forest stayed above that rate, which means it learned useful information about later movement. The important comparison is what happened when that information was turned into a queue. At every checkpoint shown, the baseline put a larger share of later declining pages near the top.

A closer look at the top 50

The difference is easier to understand at K = 50. The baseline had Precision@50 of 0.74, while the Random Forest had 0.68. In a list of 50 pages, that corresponds to about 37 later declining pages in the baseline queue and about 34 in the Random Forest queue.

Where the queues disagree

The top 50 queues share only 8 pages. So the two methods are making very different choices about which pages deserve early attention. The result does not mean that every page chosen by the baseline is good or every page chosen by the model is bad. It means that, on the observed outcome used here, the baseline makes fewer mistakes at the top of the queue.

What the model learned

The permutation importance results show that the model relied mostly on existing search visibility. Average daily impressions had the largest measured importance at 0.0844, followed by impressions at 0.0294 and CTR at 0.0139. Clicks contributed only 0.0017. Average position and the session based features added very little, while position bucket and expected CTR had negative importance in the held out evaluation.

5. Limitations and honest framing

  • Review priority is not a refresh recommendation. A page near the top deserves earlier investigation. It is not evidence that editing it will restore performance.
  • The outcome tells us what happened, not why. A fall in impressions can have several explanations, including consolidation, seasonality, changes in search results or ordinary noise.
  • The March window is short. The label compares two halves of one month, so it captures short term movement rather than a complete long term decline pattern.
  • The client holdout is useful but not universal. The main split and five fold GroupKFold check test unseen clients, but the findings still belong to this warehouse snapshot.
  • The model had signal but did not earn its extra complexity. It stayed above the base rate, yet the baseline produced the stronger queue. A more complicated method is not automatically a better method.
  • This is observational evidence. The analysis does not show that a content refresh causes recovery and does not reveal Google's ranking algorithm.

6. Ranked recommendations

The output is most useful as a working queue for a content practitioner. Start near the top, understand why the page was surfaced, check the surrounding evidence and then decide what to do.

01

Start with the baseline queue

For this dataset, the baseline is the better tested way to order the first pages for review.

02

Read the reason code before acting

A low CTR reason suggests looking at the search snippet and intent. Insufficient visibility means there may not be enough evidence for a reliable CTR decision. Unavailable position means the position comparison cannot support the ranking.

03

Diagnose before changing the page

Check for consolidation, seasonality, recent updates, search result changes and low volume. The queue tells you where to look first, not what edit to make.

04

Keep the model as supporting evidence

The Random Forest shows that the available signals contain predictive information. The evaluation does not support using it as the primary queue.
